01 / GIS / URBAN PLANNING

Knoxville’s Urban Growth Based on Terrain

An individual Spring 2026 GEOG 454 project combining terrain and planning factors to investigate housing suitability.

ArcGIS ProTerrain analysisSuitability modeling
Explore projectClose project

Context and purpose

Knoxville’s terrain creates both opportunities and constraints for housing growth. For my Spring 2026 terrain-analysis project, I investigated how slope, access to roads and existing development, environmental constraints, land cover, and zoning could be combined to identify relatively suitable areas. My goal was to build a transparent spatial model and communicate what it could tell a planning audience.

My role

This was an individual project. I carried out the GIS analysis, developed the maps, and organized the results into the StoryMap. The underlying elevation, roads, buildings, flood-zone, land-cover, boundary, and zoning datasets came from the agencies and organizations credited with the evidence.

Methods and tools

I used ArcGIS Pro to put datasets into a shared coordinate system and clip them to the study area. I derived slope from a digital elevation model and calculated distance to roads, water, and buildings. I reclassified the factors onto a common 1–5 suitability scale and combined them using weighted overlay.

Slope received the greatest emphasis, with road access and proximity to existing development also important. Gentle slopes of 0–5 degrees received high suitability. I used ArcGIS StoryMaps to connect the method to maps of terrain, accessibility, environmental constraints, and the final housing-suitability surface.

Results and limits

I produced a set of four web maps and a published StoryMap explaining the analysis. The model indicates stronger suitability where gentle terrain, road access, existing development, suitable land cover and zoning, and fewer environmental constraints coincide.

These results describe relative suitability under the model’s assumptions. They do not predict which parcels will be developed. Ownership, property values, utility capacity, developer interest, housing demand, and future zoning decisions would require further investigation before making a real planning decision.

Reflection

The main lesson I take from this project is that a suitability map reflects decisions about factors, scores, and weights. Explaining those decisions is part of making the analysis useful. This experience connects my interest in GIS with planning questions and clear public communication. My next step would be to compare alternative weights and document how sensitive the results are to those choices.

02 / GEOVISUALIZATION / WILDLIFE

The Everglade Snail Kite

An individual Spring 2026 GEOG 311 StoryMap connecting a wildlife narrative with a species-focused critical-habitat map.

ArcGIS StoryMapsWeb mappingGeographic communication
Explore projectClose project

Giving an ecological story a geographic context.

Context and purpose

For an individual project in Spring 2026 GEOG 311 Geovisualization and Geographic Information Science at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville, I created a StoryMap introducing the Everglade snail kite. I wanted to connect a short wildlife narrative to a map that would help readers locate designated critical habitat in Florida.

My role

I composed the StoryMap, selected and organized the supporting information, and configured the species-focused web map. My contribution was the geographic presentation and communication of the material. The habitat boundaries came from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the ecological information and photographs were credited to their original sources.

Methods and tools

I used ArcGIS StoryMaps to combine a brief narrative, attributed imagery, and an interactive map. I drew on information cited from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission.

In ArcGIS Map Viewer, I used the public USFWS Critical Habitat layer and filtered it to the common name “Everglade snail kite.” I saved a focused map view and embedded it in the story with its legend. This kept the mapped evidence connected to the species discussed in the text.

Results and limits

The result was a published StoryMap and an accompanying interactive habitat map. The story was first published on April 1, 2026. Together, the narrative and map communicate the subject and its geographic context.

This was a geographic communication project. The mapped boundaries are agency data; they are not new field observations, a population estimate, or a habitat model I produced. Designated critical habitat also should not be interpreted as the complete species range or as the location of individual birds or nests.

Reflection

The main lesson I take from this project is that a map and a narrative serve different but connected purposes. The text introduces the subject, while the map helps readers place it geographically. Clear source credits and careful labels help a viewer understand which parts I created and what the evidence can support. This experience strengthened my interest in using geovisualization to communicate environmental topics.

03 / WEB MAPPING / PROTOTYPE

Church Atlas

A geographic search interface for exploring sample church listings, comparing profiles, and planning a visit.

JavaScriptLeafletWeb GISInterface design
Explore projectClose project

Exploring communities through location

Church Atlas combines an interactive map with structured filters to help visitors explore sample church listings. Users can search an area, adjust a geographic radius, filter by denomination and visit needs, compare profiles, and save favorites and a visit plan in their browser.

Skills demonstrated: Geographic filtering, organizing categorical data, JavaScript interface development, and building an interactive web map with Leaflet.

Open Church Atlas demo

The prototype uses sample listings and illustrative source-status labels. Favorites and visit plans are stored locally in the browser.
